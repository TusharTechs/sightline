# Where this works, and where it does not

Sightline writes description into the gaps between dialogue. That makes it
entirely dependent on there being gaps, and how many there are is a property of
the content rather than of the software.

Measured, rather than assumed. A blind reviewer asked how it would do on other
kinds of material, so it was run over three, all public domain.

| content | length | is dialogue | describable | cues produced |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Sintel* — animated film | 14:48 | 4.5% | 90% | 24 in a 3-minute stretch |
| *Duck and Cover* — instructional, 1951 | 9:15 | 77% | 9% | 11 |
| Folgers coffee — television advert | 1:01 | 77% | 9% | **0** |

The advert produces nothing at all. Its three gaps are 3.0s, 1.5s and 1.0s, and
two of those cannot hold even the shortest usable line. That is not a failure
to fix: an advert is built to fill every second it has bought, and there is no
room in it for anything else.

The instructional film is the same 77% dialogue and still manages eleven lines,
because nine percent of nine minutes is fifty seconds while nine percent of one
minute is five. What matters is not the ratio but whether individual gaps are
long enough to hold a sentence.

## What this implies

Gap-filling has a floor, and below it the honest answer is that this approach
cannot help. For dense content the only route is the pause mode, where playback
waits for the description instead of the description squeezing between lines.
That mode exists and is not the default, and these measurements are the
argument for when it should be.

It also means a claim like "it describes video that has no description" needs
qualifying. It describes video that has no description AND has somewhere to put
it. For a film that is most of it. For an advert it is none of it.

## A note on the sources

The three items above were verified against the Internet Archive metadata API
before use: identifier exists, and the item states a public domain licence.

This mattered. The candidate list they were drawn from ran to eighteen items
and was assembled with an LLM's help. Fifteen of the eighteen identifiers did
not exist -- plausible-looking Archive.org paths, confident statements about
copyright status, and no item behind them. Only three were real. Anything
sourced that way has to be checked against the archive itself before it is
published, and the check takes a second.
